 Client-Visible Relay Diagnostics for Media over QUIC Transport (MOQT)
               draft-altanai-moq-relay-diagnostics-latest

Abstract

   Media over QUIC Transport (MOQT) deployments typically place one or
   more relays between publishers and subscribers.  When delivery
   stalls, degrades, or fails, a client observes only its own leg of the
   path, and often cannot determine whether the cause lies with the
   original publisher, an intermediate relay, path conditions,
   authorization policy, or its own behavior.

   This document collects use cases and requirements for exposing a
   limited, relay-controlled set of diagnostic information to authorized
   MOQT clients.  It examines three candidate mechanisms: an explicit
   request and response exchange on the control path, diagnostic
   information carried in existing responses and error codes, and
   advertisement of diagnostic capability during session establishment.
   Because such information can reveal deployment topology, operational
   state, or the activity of other users, authorization, data
   minimization, and relay discretion over what is disclosed are treated
   as first-order requirements rather than later refinements.

   Related operator examples describe capacity and incident observations
   within a separately authorized administrative scope.  They motivate
   correlation with existing metrics and logging systems without
   defining a relay management API.

   The goal is diagnostics that are actionable at runtime and
   interoperable across implementations, not a general-purpose telemetry
   channel within MOQT.  Information intended primarily for offline
   analysis remains the province of logging and observability systems
   such as qlog.  This document defines the problem space and the design
   constraints; a concrete wire format is left to a follow-on document
   once the working group has settled on an approach.

1.  Introduction

   Media over QUIC Transport (MOQT) [MoQTransport] distributes media
   through a publish/subscribe model in which relays sit between
   publishers and subscribers.  A subscriber therefore terminates only
   the last hop of a multi-hop path, and the signals available to it
   locally are rarely sufficient to explain why delivery is degraded or
   has failed.

   The term "diagnostics" comes from [Issue1692], which asks how a
   client can get diagnostic data from a relay.  Discussion following
   IETF 126 helped shape the use cases and requirements for any future
   wire format.

1.1.  Note to Readers

   This revision is a problem statement and requirements document.  It
   defines no protocol elements.  Comments are welcome on the MOQ
   working group mailing list.

1.3.  Terminology

   A *client-visible relay diagnostic* is a limited report of what one
   relay observed or did while handling a particular request.  For
   example, when a subscription stalls, the relay may report that it
   handed Objects to its outgoing transport for that subscription.
   Whether those Objects reached the subscriber or why playback stalled
   may remain unknown.  This document uses *operator telemetry* for the
   metrics, logs, and traces used to understand relay or service
   behavior within an authorized administrative scope.

   The transport roles follow [MoQTransport].

   *  A *publisher* publishes a Track and sends its Objects to a
      subscriber.

   *  A *subscriber* requests or accepts delivery of Objects from a
      Track.

   *  A *relay* terminates MOQT sessions and forwards Objects between
      peers.  It can act as a subscriber upstream and as a publisher
      downstream, and may cache Objects.

   This document uses the following operational roles.

   *  A *relay operator* runs a relay or a fleet of relays.

   *  A *service operator* operates a delivery service and uses
      authorized telemetry to assess delivery or investigate user
      reports.

   *  A *tenant operator* administers delivery for a tenant and can see
      the observations authorized for that tenant.

   *  A *telemetry collector* is a system that receives or retrieves
      metrics, logs, or traces for an operator.  When it subscribes to a
      MOQT telemetry Track, it acts as a subscriber for that Track.

   An organization can fill several of these roles.  For example, a
   service operator can run relays and publish content.  Each role has
   its own scope of access under the deployment's policy.

2.  Problem Statement

   MOQT clients observe only their own sessions and cannot reliably
   determine why delivery stalls or fails across a relay chain.  An
   authorized client needs a small, request-scoped account of what its
   relay observed and did, sufficient to guide waiting, retrying, or
   reporting a problem.  That account can be incomplete and cannot
   establish end-to-end delivery or identify causes outside the relay's
   knowledge.  Disclosure must protect other clients, topology, cache
   state, and operational policy, while keeping diagnostic processing
   and query rates bounded.

3.  Use Cases

   The following use cases describe the question a client or operator
   wants to answer.  They do not require a relay to disclose every fact
   that could help answer it.  In each case, a relay may provide a
   coarse, request-scoped result, withhold details, or report that it
   cannot determine the cause.

3.1.  Distinguishing a Local Request Outcome

   A subscriber issues a request for a track or a range of content and
   receives an error or observes no useful progress.  The application
   needs to distinguish between a request that was rejected or could not
   be served at this relay and a request that was accepted but has not
   yet produced content.  A useful answer might identify the outcome
   category and whether retrying the same request is likely to help.  It
   need not expose the relay's policy rules, cache keys, or internal
   request identifiers.

3.2.  Diagnosing a Delivery Stall

   A subscriber has an active subscription, but expected objects are not
   arriving within the application's tolerance.  The subscriber or its
   support tooling wants to know whether this relay has observed
   relevant upstream content, has forwarded content downstream, or
   cannot determine where progress stopped.  A bounded summary for that
   subscriber's request can guide whether to wait, retry, change tracks,
   or collect local evidence.  It should not reveal other subscribers,
   shared cache contents, or the identities of upstream peers.

3.3.  Explaining a Relay Decision

   A relay may decline, defer, or limit work for a request because of
   its local authorization or resource policy.  An authorized client
   wants an explanation that is actionable, such as whether the failure
   is likely to persist without a change in credentials or request
   parameters.  A relay can communicate a coarse outcome without
   disclosing confidential policy thresholds, tenant configuration, load
   measurements, or details that would help an attacker tune requests
   around policy.

3.4.  Supporting an Operator-Assisted Incident

   A subscriber reports a delivery problem to the service operator.  A
   diagnostic summary for the subscriber's own request or a short-lived
   correlation value can help the service operator match the report to
   the relay's handling of that request.  The value should be short-
   lived and refer only to the event, while the subscriber's access
   remains limited to its own request.  More detailed logs may be
   available to the service operator through a separately authorized
   observability system.

3.5.  Learning Whether Diagnostics Are Available

   A client may need to know whether a relay supports a diagnostic
   feature before requesting it.  A capability indication can prevent
   unsupported requests, but it should reveal only the feature category
   needed for interoperability.  It should not fingerprint a relay
   implementation or disclose deployment topology.

4.  Illustrative Diagnostic Observations

   The following proposed metrics describe one client's existing,
   authorized request.  Their names and semantics are starting points
   for discussion, not standardized MOQT fields, mandatory measurements,
   or a selected wire format.  Counter and Gauge have the numeric
   meanings described in [MoQMetrics].  A relay would disclose a metric
   only when it can attribute the observation to that request using
   bounded local state and its disclosure policy permits access.

    |                                  | request's forwarding path    |

    |                                  | checks.                      |

4.1.  Counting and Measurement Scope

   The two Counters start at zero when observation begins for the
   request and increase within one measurement epoch.  A restart, reset,
   or change of measurement scope starts a new epoch, distinguished by a
   short-lived, session-local value.  A 10-second count is the
   difference between two samples from the same epoch; a _total value is
   not a rolling 10-second count.  Samples from different epochs cannot
   be subtracted.

   The Counters use independent acceptance and handoff boundaries.  An
   Object accepted before an epoch or comparison window can complete
   handoff inside it, incrementing only the forwarding Counter for that
   interval.  Their difference is therefore not a balance and can be
   negative over a comparison window.

   An Object is identified within a request by its Full Track Name,
   Group ID, and Object ID, and contributes at most once to each Counter
   per epoch.  Incomplete Objects, status markers, duplicates, and
   transport retransmissions do not increment these Counters.  Cached
   Objects can count when accepted into or handed off for this request;
   the report does not identify their source or expose shared cache
   activity.  A relay omits a metric if bounded attribution or duplicate
   suppression is unavailable, including under subscription aggregation.

   The queue Gauge can decrease when Objects expire or are discarded, as
   well as when they are handed off.  The Counter difference therefore
   does not necessarily equal queue depth and does not establish packet
   loss or a cause.  The age Gauge uses a local monotonic clock and
   discloses no exact event timestamp.  For example, with upward
   rounding in 5-second steps, a scalar value of 5 means an age greater
   than zero and at most 5 seconds; an interval such as [1, 5] is not a
   numeric Gauge value.

4.2.  Example and Disclosure Limits

   This conceptual report samples an epoch 10 seconds after observation
   began.  Its Counter values are totals since that epoch began; later
   window counts would require comparable earlier samples.  The age
   value is rounded upward in 5-second steps.  All field names, epoch
   and precision metadata, and JSON encoding below are illustrative and
   carry no protocol allocation or interoperability requirement.

   json { "scope": "existing_request", "measurement_epoch": "session-
   local-example", "measurement_elapsed_seconds": 10, "metrics": {
   "request_objects_observed_total": 120,
   "request_objects_forwarded_total": 117, "request_queued_objects": 2,
   "request_last_forward_age_seconds": 5 }, "age_rounding_seconds": 5,
   "progress": "forwarding_observed", "cause": "unknown", "coverage":
   "partial" }

   Unsupported, unavailable, withheld, or unattributable measurements
   are omitted, not replaced with zero.  Zero represents a measured
   zero, such as no handoff counted in this epoch.  For the age Gauge,
   no known handoff means omission, not zero.  Any indication of why a
   value is absent is also subject to disclosure policy.  Approximate
   request-specific bytes forwarded or a coarse progress category, such
   as waiting or unknown, can supplement the four metrics without
   exposing peer identities or asserting an upstream cause.

   Reports exclude cache-hit status, topology, relay-wide load, and
   other users' activity.  Query rates, processing, response size, and
   retention need limits; producing a measurement must not initiate
   upstream retrieval or subscriptions.  The working group would need to
   agree these semantics, precision metadata, authorization and request
   binding, and any mapping to [MoQMetrics] before implementations could
   claim interoperability.

   For example, a relay operator can investigate scheduling or
   downstream capacity when queue bytes and delivery-timeout resets rise
   together.  A rise in failed upstream request attempts, observed where
   the relay acts as a requester, can guide examination of that relay's
   upstream connections.  High CPU or resident memory with growing load
   can motivate capacity planning or draining.  These are correlations
   to investigate, not proof of a cause or of the quality experienced by
   every viewer.

5.1.  Aggregation and Access Boundaries

   Every operator sample needs an administrative scope, collection
   interval or capture time, freshness, and coverage.  Counters have
   local measurement epochs; rates or window deltas are calculated per
   relay before aggregation so restarts are not mistaken for traffic
   changes.  Gauges are combined only across disjoint scopes with
   compatible meanings.  Fleet reports disclose missing or stale
   contributors instead of presenting partial coverage as a complete
   service view.  Fleet CPU fractions are weighted by the disclosed
   capacity basis rather than summed.  Capacity thresholds and alert
   policies remain deployment decisions.

   Operator authorization is role- and scope-specific.  A tenant
   operator sees only that tenant's authorized observations; node-wide
   CPU, shared-cache inventory, or another tenant's activity needs
   separate permission.  Collector access, retention, and incident
   correlation need auditing and access controls.  Labels use bounded
   categories such as request type and outcome, avoiding tokens, user
   identifiers, full content names, or arbitrary peer addresses.
   Detailed logs or traces are retrieved only for an authorized, bounded
   incident context, with sensitive fields minimized.  These operational
   views are not returned through an ordinary client's private
   diagnostic view.

6.  Requirements

   These requirements describe design goals for a future mechanism.
   They do not specify MOQT messages or require relays to expose
   operational information.  Requirements to identify an individual
   request or report from one relay apply to the client-visible view.
   Operator aggregates instead identify their administrative scope,
   contributing relays, coverage, and freshness as described above; they
   retain the applicable access and resource limits.

6.1.  Diagnostic Scope

   *  A mechanism needs to identify the request or delivery context to
      which a diagnostic applies, without requiring a stable identifier
      that can be used to correlate a client's activity across sessions.

   *  A diagnostic needs to distinguish, where the relay has sufficient
      evidence, between a relay-observed outcome and an unknown or
      unobserved cause.

   *  A mechanism needs to permit an implementation to return no
      diagnostic or a less detailed result when the relay lacks reliable
      evidence.

   *  A diagnostic needs to avoid implying facts about upstream
      endpoints or network segments that the reporting relay cannot
      observe.

6.2.  Privacy and Authorization

   *  A relay needs to authenticate and authorize access to diagnostics
      according to its deployment's policy.  A client authorized to
      request content is not automatically authorized to inspect general
      relay health or other clients' activity.

   *  Diagnostic responses need to be scoped to the requesting client's
      own session and request, except where a separately defined and
      authorized operator interface is used.

   *  Operator collection needs explicit administrative and tenant
      scope, role-based access, bounded retention, and auditability.
      Authorization for content delivery or a client diagnostic view
      does not grant operator access.

   *  A mechanism needs to support data minimization: disclose the least
      information useful for the stated diagnostic purpose, and allow
      the relay to omit sensitive fields.

   *  Diagnostics need not disclose relay topology, upstream identities,
      cache keys or contents, tenant names, policy rules, precise
      resource utilization, or information about other clients.

   *  Any correlation value needs to be scoped, short-lived, and
      difficult to use as a cross-session or cross-service tracking
      identifier.

   *  A client needs to be able to distinguish unsupported, unavailable,
      and withheld information where doing so does not itself create an
      unacceptable information leak.  A mechanism can deliberately
      collapse these outcomes when policy requires it.

6.3.  Safety and Operational Behavior

   *  A mechanism needs bounded response size and bounded processing
      cost.

   *  A relay needs discretion to rate-limit, defer, or refuse
      diagnostic requests without affecting ordinary content delivery.

   *  Diagnostic requests need to avoid causing new upstream
      subscriptions, cache fills, or other expensive work solely to
      generate a diagnostic.

   *  A diagnostic needs to be clearly identified as a point-in-time
      report from one relay.  It may be incomplete, delayed, or based on
      an approximation.

   *  Diagnostics need not be enabled by default.  Capability discovery
      and use should be compatible with deployments that choose not to
      expose them.

   *  Clients need to treat diagnostic information as advisory and
      continue to apply their own timeouts, retry limits, and security
      policy.

7.  Candidate Mechanisms

7.1.  Explicit Request and Response on the Control Path

   An explicit request lets a client ask for a diagnostic when a
   particular request is stalled or has failed.  It can carry a request-
   scoped context and allow the relay to return a result only when
   asked.  This avoids sending diagnostics for every request and gives
   the relay an opportunity to apply authorization and rate limits.

   The mechanism also adds a new request/response exchange and
   associated state.  It needs rules for matching a diagnostic to a
   request, handling unavailable or withheld information, and preventing
   repeated queries from becoming a resource or probing attack.  A
   diagnostic request must not trigger expensive upstream work solely to
   answer the query.

7.2.  Diagnostic Information in Existing Responses and Error Codes

   Adding a small diagnostic category to an existing response or error
   can make an outcome immediately actionable and avoid a separate round
   trip.  It is a natural fit when the relay is already returning a
   result for the request.

   This approach is less suitable for later investigation of an ongoing
   stall, and risks turning response fields into a channel for detailed
   operational data.  Error details may also be propagated or cached by
   relays.  Any details would therefore need to remain compact, request-
   specific, privacy-preserving, and safe to forward or retain for the
   lifetime defined by the base protocol.

7.3.  Capability Advertisement During Session Establishment

   Capability advertisement can tell a client whether a relay
   understands a diagnostic mechanism before the client uses it.  It can
   support gradual deployment and avoid sending requests that cannot be
   understood.

   An advertisement says that a capability is supported; it does not
   authorize a particular client or guarantee that a relay will disclose
   a diagnostic for a particular request.  Detailed capability lists can
   fingerprint implementations or deployments, so discovery should
   identify only the protocol feature needed for interoperability.

   The mechanisms are not necessarily exclusive.  A later design could
   use capability discovery together with either an explicit query or
   compact response details.  Selection requires working-group agreement
   on the target use cases and privacy model.

8.  Relationship to Other Work

   This work concerns request-scoped explanations that can help an
   endpoint make a runtime decision or help a user report a problem.  It
   is not a general metrics format, a relay management API, or a logging
   protocol.

   [MoQMetrics] provides a numeric data model, live subscriptions, and
   retrieval of cached measurements.  Its Counter and Gauge types are
   candidates for the observations illustrated here.  The proposed
   request semantics, attribution, and disclosure rules would need an
   agreed mapping, including naming and authorization, rather than a
   competing metrics transport.

   [MoQLog] provides structured operational logging over MOQT, while
   [QLOG] provides event representations useful for protocol analysis
   and incident correlation.  Existing metrics, logs, and traces can
   support either audience when authorized.  The operator examples here
   motivate reuse of those systems; they do not define a replacement
   collector, dashboard, or management API.

   The following are out of scope: exposing a relay's complete internal
   state; distributing network-wide or cross-tenant telemetry to
   ordinary clients; proving end-to-end delivery; standardizing operator
   dashboards; and defining a general-purpose query language for relay
   state.

9.  Security Considerations

   Relay diagnostics create a deliberate information channel from a
   relay to a client.  The channel can reveal operational state,
   deployment relationships, traffic patterns, and policy decisions.  An
   attacker may use repeated or carefully varied requests to infer
   whether content is cached, whether another client is active, how a
   tenant is configured, or when a relay is under load.  Even coarse
   fields can become identifying when combined with timing and client-
   side observations.

   A future protocol design should apply data minimization at the relay,
   not rely only on clients to protect received data.  Diagnostics
   should be tied to the requesting client's authorization context and
   scoped to that client's own request.  General relay health, cross-
   client statistics, and operator correlation should use separately
   authorized interfaces.  Deployments may choose not to provide client-
   visible diagnostics or may provide only a small set of coarse
   outcomes.

   Operator observations need separately scoped authorization, tenant
   isolation, access auditing, and retention controls.  Aggregation does
   not by itself make data anonymous, and small groups or repeated
   queries can still reveal an individual client's activity.
   Correlation with detailed logs or traces must remain within the
   operator's authorized incident scope.

   Implementations should avoid returning stable relay or client
   identifiers, precise timestamps, exact cache keys, upstream
   addresses, internal error text, or raw policy and resource
   measurements unless a separate, explicit trust model justifies them.
   Any event correlation value should be short-lived and non-reusable
   across sessions.  A relay may deliberately make "unknown",
   "withheld", and "not available" indistinguishable if differentiating
   them would expose sensitive policy or state.

   Diagnostic queries can consume CPU, memory, or control-stream
   capacity, and may be abused to probe policy or amplify traffic.
   Responses and processing need limits; relays need rate limiting and
   discretion to refuse queries.  Generating a diagnostic should not
   trigger content retrieval, cache filling, or upstream subscription
   work.  Where diagnostic messages can be replayed, cached, or
   propagated by other relays, their scope and lifetime need to be
   defined so that one client's result is not disclosed to another.

   Clients should treat diagnostics as advisory, potentially incomplete
   observations.  They should not infer that a named upstream component
   is at fault based solely on a relay response, and should not use the
   absence of a diagnostic as proof that delivery is healthy.  Operators
   should consider the privacy of diagnostic data in logs, support
   tickets, and telemetry pipelines.
